# Module: Difficult emails (Patterson et al., Crucial Conversations)

Load for bad news, saying no, feedback, apologies, escalations, conflict, or owning a missed commitment.

## Why this module exists

Difficult emails go wrong in two ways: they get vague to avoid conflict (the reader misses the news), or they get blunt and the reader gets defensive (the relationship takes the hit). *Crucial Conversations* is about conversations where stakes are high, opinions differ, and emotions run strong. Its answer is to keep the facts clear and the reader feeling safe at the same time. The STATE method is its recipe for saying something hard.

## Before drafting: start with heart

Answer three questions privately, then write toward them:

1. What do I really want for myself?
2. What do I want for the reader?
3. What do I want for the relationship?

If the draft does not serve all three, rewrite it.

## STATE, in email form

| Step | What it means | Email move |
|---|---|---|
| **S**hare your facts | Start with what is observable and least contestable | "We will not build custom field mapping in Q2. It is not on the Q2 roadmap." |
| **T**ell your story | Then your conclusion or reason, clearly labelled as yours | "My read is that a one-off build for one prospect would push back the Salesforce integration that every sales team is waiting on." |
| **A**sk for others' paths | Invite their view and facts | "What am I missing about how much this prospect depends on it?" |
| **T**alk tentatively | State your story as a view, not a verdict, without hedging the facts | "My read is", "it looks to me like". Facts stay firm. |
| **E**ncourage testing | Make it safe to disagree | "If the numbers say otherwise, I want to see them." |

Facts come first because they are the least likely to start an argument. The reason comes second, framed as yours, so the reader can push on it without feeling attacked.

## Make it safe

- **Mutual purpose.** Name the goal you share in one line: "We both want this deal closed."
- **Contrasting.** When the reader may hear more than you mean, say what you do not mean, then what you do: "I am not saying the prospect does not matter. I am saying a one-off build is not the way to win them." Use it once, where misreading is likely.
- **An alternative where one exists.** Saying no lands better with a yes to something else: a workaround, a later date, a partial version.

## Bad news, apology, and feedback specifics

- **Bad news:** the news in line one. Do not open with a warm-up; the reader feels set up.
- **Apology:** own it in one sentence without "if" or "but" ("I missed the date I gave you."), then the impact on them, the fix, and what changes so it does not recur.
- **Feedback:** describe the specific situation and behaviour and its impact, not the person's character. Keep praise and criticism in separate sentences.
- **Escalation:** state what has been tried, the decision needed, and by when. No blame language.

## Email may be the wrong channel

The book's methods were built for live conversation. When the stakes are high and emotions are strong (a performance issue, a broken commitment to a client, a conflict that is already heated), recommend a short call first and use the email to confirm what was agreed. Say this in one line under the draft and still provide the draft.

## Pass check

- [ ] The hard news or "no" is in the first two lines, not buried.
- [ ] Facts come before interpretation; interpretation is labelled as a view.
- [ ] At least one line invites the reader's view or facts.
- [ ] Shared purpose is stated; an alternative is offered where one exists.
- [ ] No blame, sarcasm, or character judgements. No hedging of the facts themselves.
