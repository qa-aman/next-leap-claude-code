# Module: Follow-ups and negotiation (Voss, Never Split the Difference)

Load for chasers when someone has gone quiet, and for emails that negotiate or push back: scope, timelines, resourcing, priorities, a request you cannot fully meet.

## Why this module exists

"Just checking in" and "circling back" invite more silence, because they ask for nothing and give the reader no reason to reply. Chris Voss, a former FBI hostage negotiator, built his method on making the other side feel safe and in control. In email that means: questions that are easy to answer, especially with "no", and naming the other person's likely concern before they have to.

## Techniques, in email form

**No-oriented questions.** People reply more readily to a question they can answer "no" to, because saying no feels safe. Phrase the ask so "no" moves things forward. Aim the question at the substance (the decision, the date, the scope), never at the act of asking: "Is it unrealistic to have the API ready by May?" works, "Is it a bad idea to ask you for an answer?" reads as passive-aggressive.

- "Is it a bad idea to lock the API scope at Friday's planning?"
- "Would it be unreasonable to ask for a yes or no by Thursday?"
- For a thread that has gone fully silent, the one-line version: "Have you given up on [project]?" Use it only after at least two unanswered follow-ups, and only with peers or partners, never with someone senior on a first chase. It is blunt by design.

**Labels.** Name the other person's situation or concern in one line, starting "It seems like", "It sounds like", or "It looks like". It shows you understand their position without arguing with it.

- "It seems like Q2 is already overloaded for your team."

**Accusation audit.** Say the negative thing they might be thinking before they think it. It takes the sting out of a hard ask.

- "This will probably feel like one more ask in a week full of them."

**Calibrated questions.** Open "how" or "what" questions that hand the problem to them without a fight, useful when pushing back.

- "How would you like me to handle the two features that do not fit in the sprint?"
- "What would it take to get the API by May?"

## Shape of a follow-up

1. Keep the original subject so the thread history is attached.
2. Line 1: the ask, restated in one sentence, ideally as a no-oriented question.
3. Line 2: a label or accusation audit (optional, one line).
4. Line 3: why the date matters to them or to the shared goal, and the deadline.
5. Make the reply one word or one line: "A yes or no is enough."

Escalate gently over successive chasers: first a reminder with the ask, then a label plus the no-oriented question, then the one-line "Have you given up on..." if the relationship allows it.

## Shape of a push-back

Lead with what you can do, label their need, state what you cannot do and why in one line, then hand over a calibrated question or two concrete options. Never split the difference by default; offer a real trade-off.

## Pass check

- [ ] No "just checking in", "circling back", "bumping this", or "following up on my last email".
- [ ] The ask is answerable in one word or one line.
- [ ] At most one label and one accusation audit, so it does not read as a script.
- [ ] Tone matches seniority: no "Have you given up" to an exec on a first chase.
